\chapter{The plain sieve}\label{chap:sieve}

Stage~C1 replaces the brute-force reference in the program by a sieve in the skeleton of the C
function \verb|find_points_work_1|, with the simplest component in each place: the denominators
$1, \dots, H$ in order, the numerators $-H, \dots, H$, one Boolean per numerator, prime moduli
with tables of admissible residues, the test for common factors, the exact check, the delivery of
$\pm y$, and the points at infinity computed directly from $d$ and $c_d$. Every row of the sieve is
\emph{sound}: it lets every point through. So for every well-formed choice of the sieving
parameters the sieve returns exactly the list of the brute-force reference, the same points in the
same order, and membership and no duplicates follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:bruteForce}.

The later stages contribute in four ways. A \emph{data refinement} computes the same predicate in
another way, with a lemma that it does: the bit arrays of words of stage~C2, the faithful tables of
stage~F1, the stages of the sieve and the check of stage~F2, whose third stage tests the rows of
moduli that the sieve applies already, the packing of stage~F3, and the chunked first stage and the
rows of the builders of stage~F4; the equation with the reference survives it. A
\emph{transformation}, the reversal of stage~D, is a bijection of the point sets that preserves the
box. A \emph{sound filter} comes with the lemma that every point passes it: the 2-adic information
and the tests on the denominators of stage~E and the composite moduli of stage~F1. An
\emph{enumeration refinement} visits the denominators that carry points in another order: the loop
shapes of stage~E. From the reversal on, and with the divisor order of stage~E, the statement is
that the output is a permutation of the reference, which still gives membership and no duplicates;
since stage~E a theorem also gives its order, that of the reference over the denominators in the
order of the loop of the search (Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_order}), which stage~G spells out in
terms of the polynomial (Theorem~\ref{thm:order}). Since stage~I the search chooses its moduli and
the cut points of its stages from the curve and corrects them between the blocks of the run, as
ratpoints does (Chapter~\ref{chap:moduli}); the rows of every modulus that it may choose are sound,
so the theorems hold for every choice of the moduli that the search makes.

\section{What a default run of the C code does}\label{sec:skeleton}

\verb|find_points| (\csource{find_points.c}{5226}{5242}) calls \verb|find_points_work|, which
refuses a degree outside $0, \dots, \mathrm{maxDegree}$ (\csource{find_points.c}{4557}{4558}) and
calls
\verb|find_points_work_1|. In a run without options, that function:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item drops the leading zero coefficients, refusing the form as not squarefree when that lowers
    $\lceil n/2 \rceil$, refuses degree $0$ and a height below $1$, and caps the parameters
    (\csource{find_points.c}{4624}{4658}); it reverses the polynomial in some cases, refusing the
    form as not squarefree for even $d$ with $c_0 = c_1 = 0$ (\csource{find_points.c}{4732}{4803};
    stage~D);
  \item computes the remainder chain of $f$ and $f'$ of the Sturm step, refusing the form as not
    squarefree when a remainder is zero (\csource{sturm.c}{99}{163},
    \csource{find_points.c}{4857}{4892}; stage~D), and from it the search intervals of the search
    region where $f$ may be $\ge 0$ (stage~H, Chapter~\ref{chap:region}), and decides whether there
    are points at infinity (\csource{find_points.c}{4894}{4899});
  \item sets up the tests on the denominators (squares or divisors times squares, Jacobi symbols)
    and the 2-adic information, with an exit when no class of denominators modulo $64$ admits a
    numerator (stage~E);
  \item chooses the moduli and the forbidden divisors, examining each prime: the table
    \verb|is_f_square| of the residues $x$ modulo $p$ for which $f(x)$ is a square modulo $p$
    (\csource{find_points.c}{1703}{1747}), with an exit when there are no points modulo $p$
    (stage~D);
  \item delivers the points at infinity (\csource{find_points.c}{5068}{5105});
  \item runs over the denominators in blocks (\csource{find_points.c}{5152}{5160}); for each
    denominator $b$ that passes its tests, it sieves the numerators of the search region in pieces
    of bit arrays with the rows of the moduli of the first two stages (\verb|sift| and
    \verb|_ratpoints_sift0|), tests each survivor for common factors with $b$
    (\csource{sift.c}{318}{343}), then with the primes of the third stage one numerator at a time,
    and checks the rest exactly, delivering $(a, y, b)$ and, for $y \ne 0$, $(a, -y, b)$
    (\csource{find_points.c}{5250}{5338}).
\end{enumerate}
C1 models what decides which points are found, each in its simplest form. Most of the rest only
spares work; the refusal of a form that is not squarefree, the reversal and the exit when there
are no points modulo $p$ (D) and the options of the output (G) come in their stages.

\begin{definition}[Sieving parameters]\label{def:Params}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Params}
  \leanok
  The free choices of the sieve: the cut points $s_1 \le s_2$, positions in the sieve entries of the
  choice of the moduli, which end the first and the second stage; at most how many forbidden
  divisors are tested; whether the Jacobi test may run; and at most how many bit arrays a piece of
  the numerators has, the field \verb|array_size| of the arguments of the library
  (\csource{ratpoints.h}{368}{368}; Definition~\ref{def:arraySize}). The Jacobi switch stands for
  the bit \verb|RATPOINTS_NO_JACOBI| of the flags (\csource{ratpoints.h}{406}{406}), on when the bit
  is clear. As the result of the choice of the moduli (Definition~\ref{def:choice}) the parameters
  also have the end $s_3$ of the third stage, the moduli after it being the reserve of the
  correction of the moduli, the number of primes looked at in all, the estimates of the words and
  the denominators of the run, the cost of a check relative to the reference and the number of words
  at which the first correction is made (\csource{ratpoints.h}{395}{397}), which the run of the
  search reads: the end of the third stage as that of the first decision, and the estimates, the
  cost and the words for the correction of the moduli between its blocks
  (Definition~\ref{def:correction}). The parameters are internal to the search, the result of the
  choice of the moduli that it makes from the curve with the options of the sieve
  (Definition~\ref{def:choice}), whose contract states what the run needs of them
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:choice}), so that the parameters need no well-formedness of their own.
\end{definition}

The choice of the moduli makes the choices of the sieve, the sieve entries and \verb|Params|: which
moduli the sieve uses, in which order and in which stage, and how many primes are examined.
ratpoints makes them from the curve (\verb|sieving_info|, which ranks the primes and may look past
the first $q$ when \verb|-p| is absent), and moves the end of the third stage during the run
(\verb|adapt_primes|; with \verb|-A 2| also $s_2$), and so does the search of the model, with the
choice and the correction of Chapter~\ref{chap:moduli}. None of this changes the points found or
their order, since a modulus only removes candidates that are not points, a test on the denominators
(the forbidden divisors among the examined primes and beyond them, at most the bound of them, and
the Jacobi test that the switch lets run) removes only denominators without points, and the two
shapes of the loop between which these choices decide, the words of 64 denominators and every
denominator, both run in increasing order (\verb|-q| suppresses the report of the moduli used, which
does depend on the choice). So the model makes the choice inside the search, as the C code does, and
the theorems hold for every value of the options of the sieve, whatever moduli the search chooses:
the proofs read of the result of the choice only what its contract states (Lemma~\ref{lem:choice}).
Until stage~I the theorems quantified over the well-formed parameters, the comparator challenge
importing \verb|Params.WF|, and \verb|wf_sieveParams|, certified with them, showed that every
parameter set the command line produces is well-formed, which ruled out a well-formedness that
nothing satisfies. C1 reads only the moduli and treats all stages alike, and C2 reads the array size
too (Definition~\ref{def:arraySize}); the bound on the forbidden divisors and the Jacobi switch are
read since stage~E, the number of primes examined since stage~F1 (Chapter~\ref{chap:tables}), the
cut points from stage~F2 on, and the end of the third stage, the estimates of the run, the cost of a
check and the words of the first correction, which the correction of the moduli reads, since
stage~I. Since then the moduli come to the run as their sieve entries, beside the parameters, and
the number of primes examined stays inside the choice of the moduli, as \verb|pn_lim| stays inside
\verb|sieving_info|. The constants of the build that the table, the parameters and the options of
the sieve read, the size in bits of the moduli and the defaults of \verb|-p| and \verb|-F|, are
definitions of the model like $\mathrm{maxDegree}$ (Definition~\ref{def:maxDegree}), and no proof
depends on their values (Definition~\ref{def:choiceConstants}).

\section{The table of a prime}\label{sec:primeTables}

Write $f = \sum_{i \le d} c_i x^i$ for the polynomial of degree $d$. For a prime $p$ and a
denominator $b$ not divisible by $p$, $F_c(a, b) \equiv b^D f(a b^{-1}) \pmod p$, and $b^D$ is the
square of a unit since $D$ is even; so if $F_c(a, b) = y^2$, then $f(a b^{-1})$ is a square
modulo $p$ (zero included). If $p > 1$ divides $b$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then $p$ does not divide
$a$. These two facts make sound the rows that the tables below define.

\begin{definition}[Squares and inverses modulo a prime]\label{def:squaresMod}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.squaresMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.inversesMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_squaresMod_mul_self_mod, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_inversesMod_mul_mod}
  \leanok
  The table of the squares modulo $p$, made as the generator of the tables of ratpoints makes it:
  zero, and the squares of the odd residues (\csource{gen_find_points_h.c}{69}{88}); and the
  table of the inverses modulo $p$ of the residues $1, \dots, p - 1$, each the least positive one
  found by trial (\csource{gen_find_points_h.c}{33}{44}, \csource{gen_find_points_h.c}{90}{100}):
  the rows of $p$ in the tables \verb|squares| and \verb|inverses| that the build generates and
  \verb|find_points.c| includes (\csource{find_points.c}{37}{37}). The squares of all residues are
  marked, and $r \cdot r^{-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ for a prime $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The table of a prime]\label{def:examinePrime}
  \uses{def:squaresMod}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.evalMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.Entry, Ratpoints.Sieve.examinePrime, Ratpoints.Sieve.natCast_evalMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_isFSquare_examinePrime_of_isSquare, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_isFSquare_examinePrime_self}
  \leanok
  For a prime $p$: for each residue $x$ modulo $p$ whether $f(x)$ is a square modulo $p$, with
  $f(x)$ computed by Horner's rule modulo $p$ from the coefficients reduced modulo $p$ by the
  Euclidean remainder, and as the entry $p$ whether there is a point at infinity modulo $p$, for odd
  $d$ or $c_d$ a square modulo $p$: $p + 1$ entries, as \verb|examine_prime| keeps them; kept, as
  the sieve entry of ratpoints keeps it (\csource{rp-private.h}{542}{555}), together with $p$ and
  the inverses modulo $p$. The table is made for every prime it is given. It is the reference of the
  table with the exit (Definition~\ref{def:examinePrimeExit}), which models \verb|examine_prime|
  with its two paths through \verb|long|s and GMP integers: that gives no table at a prime modulo
  which the curve has no points, and this one at every other prime below $\mathrm{maxPrimeEven}$.
  Ratpoints also makes no table for a prime at which $f(x)$ is a square for every residue and there
  is no point at infinity modulo $p$, which it leaves out of the moduli.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The rows of a denominator]\label{def:admits}
  \uses{def:examinePrime}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.Entry.admits}
  \leanok
  The row of the denominator $b$ in the table of $p$ admits the numerator $a$ when, for the residues
  $r = b \bmod p$ and $x = a \bmod p$, the table's entry at $x r^{-1} \bmod p$ is true if $r \ne 0$,
  and $x \ne 0$ if $r = 0$. This is what the bit of $a$ says in the row of $r$ in ratpoints, where
  the rows are bit patterns built from the table by a function for each prime that the build
  generates (Definitions~\ref{def:sieveInit1} and~\ref{def:sieveInit2}) and, for $r = 0$, a fixed
  pattern that does not depend on the curve (Definition~\ref{def:sieves0}); the admission test, with
  no C range of its own, is what these rows are specified by, through the closed form
  (Definition~\ref{def:closedRow}). The table of a prime is the one factor of the sieve
  entry of the prime (Definition~\ref{def:SieveEntry}), whose row is this row; a numerator survives
  the moduli for $b$ when the row of $b$ in the sieve entry of every modulus admits it, the
  interface between the sieve and the check, against which the bit arrays of stage~C2 are specified
  (Chapter~\ref{chap:words}).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[A square value of the form gives a square value of $f$]\label{lem:isSquare_div}
  \uses{def:binaryForm}
  \lean{IsUnit.isSquare_of_sq_eq_mul_pow, Ratpoints.sum_mul_pow_mul_pow_sub_of_eq_mul, Ratpoints.isSquare_sum_mul_pow_of_sq_eq_binaryForm, Ratpoints.isSquare_sum_mul_div_pow_of_sq_eq}
  \leanok
  In a commutative monoid, if $y^2 = v u^D$ with $u$ a unit and $D$ even, then $v$ is a square. In a
  commutative semiring, if $d \le D$ and $a = k b$, then
  $\sum_{i \le d} f_i\, a^i b^{D - i} = b^D \sum_{i \le d} f_i\, k^i$. So if $y^2 = F_c(a, b)$ for
  integers $a$, $y$ and a natural number $b$ whose image in a commutative ring $R$ is a unit, then
  $f(k)$ is a square in $R$ for the $k$ with $a = k b$ in $R$. In a semifield likewise, if
  $b \ne 0$, $D$ is even, $d \le D$ and $y^2 = \sum_{i \le d} f_i\, a^i b^{D - i}$, then
  $\sum_{i \le d} f_i\, (a/b)^i$ is a square.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The form is $b^D \sum_{i \le d} f_i\, k^i$, and $b^D$ is the square of the unit $b^{D/2}$, so the
  sum is the square of $y b^{-D/2}$.
\end{proof}

Mathlib has the identity behind this in the form of polynomials, \verb|Polynomial.eval_homogenize|;
the sum form, with its few lines of proof, suits the definition of $F_c$.

\begin{lemma}[The rows let every point through]\label{lem:admits_examinePrime}
  \uses{def:admits, def:binaryForm, def:bruteForce}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.admits_examinePrime_of_sq_eq}
  \leanok
  Let $p$ be a prime, $b$ a natural number, $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $y^2 = F_c(a, b)$. Then the row
  of $b$ in the table of $p$ for the degree of $c$ admits $a$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{lem:isSquare_div}
  \leanok
  The tables are what they claim to be: the squares, the inverses and Horner's rule modulo $p$
  compute their definitions. For $p \nmid b$, cast $y^2 = F_c(a, b)$ to $\ZZ/p\ZZ$ and apply
  Lemma~\ref{lem:isSquare_div}; the table of squares then has the entry at $a b^{-1}$. For
  $p \mid b$, a common divisor $p > 1$ of $a$ and $b$ would divide $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, so
  $p \nmid a$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:admits_examinePrime} needs neither $b \ge 1$ nor an odd prime, so at its use the
sieve's denominators and the primes of the table need no side conditions.

\section{The sieve}\label{sec:sieve}

The model follows the rules of the translation throughout. A C \verb|long| is an \verb|Int| or a
\verb|Nat|, except in the test for common factors and in the bits of an interval of numerators,
which compute on the 64-bit words of Lean core as the C code does (Definitions~\ref{def:relprime}
and~\ref{def:numeratorsToBits}, Lemmas~\ref{lem:relprime} and~\ref{lem:numeratorsToBits}); the
reductions by a reciprocal cast their arguments to 64-bit words, as the macros cast them to
\verb|unsigned long|s, and take the high half of the 128-bit product from the products of the 32-bit
halves (Definitions~\ref{def:mulMod} and~\ref{def:mulHi}); the obligation that a value lies in the
range of its type is a lemma of the faithful component that computes in \verb|long|s: since stage~D
for the helper \verb|mod| and the negations of the map of a reversed search
(Definition~\ref{def:useCLong}), and from stage~F on for the faithful components of the sieve (in
stage~F2 the bounds of the third stage, Lemma~\ref{lem:stage3}, and in stage~F3 those of the
packing, Lemma~\ref{lem:packingLongs}). The remainder \verb|mod()| of the C code is Lean's Euclidean
\verb|%|, but in the table of a prime with the exit and the tables of a prime power, which model it
as it is and prove it equal to \verb|%| (Definitions~\ref{def:useCLong} and~\ref{def:examinePower}).
The tables that the build of ratpoints generates (the squares, the inverses, the row of $0$) are
Lean functions with lemmas that they compute what they claim, checked against the generated header
by \verb|#guard| examples; the row of $0$ is the closed form of its admission test
(Lemma~\ref{lem:sieves0}). Each loop of the sieve is a definition of type \verb|Id α| that its
callers bind with \verb|←|, so that its contract, an \verb|@[spec]| theorem proved with the program
logic of Lean core and with loop invariants that are named definitions, applies at the call; only
$\mathrm{findPoints}$, $\mathrm{findPointsWith}$ and $\mathrm{findPointsVerbose}$, the program's run
and its pure callback, \verb|Main.lean| and the \verb|#guard| lines may run a component with
\verb|Id.run|. The reference table of a prime (Definition~\ref{def:examinePrime}) is made of folds
and maps, specified by ordinary lemmas; the table with the exit and the tables of a prime power,
which loop over the residues, are components of type \verb|Id| with their contracts
(Lemmas~\ref{lem:examinePrime_eq} and~\ref{lem:power_admits}).

\begin{definition}[The sieve of one denominator]\label{def:sieveDen}
  \uses{def:SieveEntry}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.sieveDen, Ratpoints.Sieve.sieveDen_spec}
  \leanok
  An array of $2H + 1$ Booleans, one per numerator $a = i - H$, all true at the start; for the sieve
  entry of each modulus in turn, every entry is replaced by its conjunction with the row of $b$ at
  $a$. Its contract: entry $i$ is the survivor test of the numerator $i - H$. These are the ANDs of
  the first and the second stage of ratpoints, with the masks at the ends of the range, and the test
  of its third stage, in their simplest form; with the check of Definition~\ref{def:checkDen} the
  sieve stays in the model as the reference of the bit arrays of words of Chapter~\ref{chap:words},
  which model that code (Lemma~\ref{lem:sift_eq_checkDen}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The survivors of one denominator]\label{def:checkDen}
  \uses{def:bruteForce}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.checkDen, Ratpoints.Sieve.checkDen_spec}
  \leanok
  The numerators whose entry in a given array of Booleans is true, in increasing order, each tested
  for common factors with $b$ and checked exactly: $F_c(a, b)$ by Horner's rule on the products
  $c_k b^{d-k}$, computed once per denominator, times $b$ once more when $d$ is odd, then
  $(a, y, b)$ and, for $y \ne 0$, $(a, -y, b)$ when $F_c(a, b) = y^2$ with $y \ge 0$; the exact
  check is that of the brute-force reference. Its contract: if entry $i$ is $k(i - H)$ for every
  $i$, for a test $k$ of the numerators, then the result is what the brute-force reference finds
  over the denominator $b$ and the numerators $-H, \dots, H$ that pass $k$. These are the scan and
  the extraction of ratpoints with the test for common factors and the exact check, in their
  simplest form; on the sieve of Definition~\ref{def:sieveDen} the check stays in the model as the
  reference of the bit arrays of words of Chapter~\ref{chap:words}, which model that code
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:sift_eq_checkDen}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The denominators]\label{def:sieveRun}
  \uses{def:runBlock, def:correction}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.sieveRun}
  \leanok
  The run over the blocks of the shape of the loop (Definition~\ref{def:Shape}) from
  $b_{\mathrm{low}}$ to $b_{\mathrm{high}}$ (Definition~\ref{def:blockPlan}): from the start of the
  correction of the moduli, with the cut points of the result of the choice as the first decision
  and the words of the first correction (Definition~\ref{def:correction}), for each block in turn
  one block (Definition~\ref{def:runBlock}), with the decision of the correction in force for it,
  the end $s_1$ of the first stage, the sieve entries of the moduli with their reciprocals, the row
  offsets of the classes, the word \verb|den_bits|, the bounds of the search intervals of the run
  (Definition~\ref{def:exactBounds}) and pieces of at most $A$ bit arrays, the store of the tables
  of rows (Definition~\ref{def:entryRef}) passed from one block to the next, and the fold of its
  report into the correction, the run returning after a block in which the callback stopped the
  search; it returns the delivery with the ends $s_2$ and $s_3$ of the last block folded, which the
  search writes back (\csource{find_points.c}{5120}{5120}, \csource{find_points.c}{5152}{5160},
  \csource{find_points.c}{5163}{5163}). Its contract, the block lemma, is Theorem~\ref{thm:blocks}:
  the run delivers the points of its blocks in order, each sifted with the moduli of its decision, a
  function of the block that depends neither on the callback nor on the flags of the output; per
  denominator, the points of the check with the flags of the numerators in its ranges that pass the
  word of its class and survive the moduli of the three stages of the decision of its block. The
  pool of threads with which ratpoints may sift the blocks, which the model leaves out, gives the
  same output (Theorem~\ref{thm:threads}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The points at infinity]\label{def:pointsAtInfinity}
  \uses{def:delivery, def:reversal}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.pointsAtInfinity}
  \leanok
  For the callback, the flags of the check and a delivery whose flag is clear
  (Definition~\ref{def:delivery}): when there are points at infinity, for odd $d$ or $c_d$ a square
  (the square test of the reversal check, Definition~\ref{def:reversal}), with the candidate
  $(a, b) = (1, 0)$, or $(0, 1)$ when the flag of the reversal is set, as in the C code after a
  reversal, one call with $(a : 0 : b)$ under $\mathrm{noCheck}$ or for odd $d$, and for even $d$
  the call with $(a : s : b)$ for $s = \lfloor \sqrt{c_d} \rfloor$ and then, unless that call
  stopped the search or $\mathrm{noY}$, the call with $(a : -s : b)$, with no test $s \ne 0$, as the
  C code has none; nothing when $d$ is even and $c_d$ is not a square
  (\csource{find_points.c}{4634}{4634}, \csource{find_points.c}{4894}{4896},
  \csource{find_points.c}{4899}{4899}, \csource{find_points.c}{5068}{5074},
  \csource{find_points.c}{5078}{5099}); the return after a stop at infinity is made by the search
  after the choice of the moduli (Definition~\ref{def:chooseAndSearch}), which also prints the line
  before the points under \verb|-v|, the line that there are points at infinity coming with the
  report of the tests on the denominators (Definition~\ref{def:report}). For odd $d$ or $c_d \ne 0$,
  with the flags of the output clear and a callback that does not stop the search, the calls are
  those of the points of the candidate $(1, 0)$ of the brute-force reference, mapped by
  $\mathrm{swapPoint}$ (Definition~\ref{def:swapPoint}) when the flag of the reversal is set: after
  a reversal the points $(0, y, 1)$ of the caller's curve (Lemma~\ref{lem:checkNumWith}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The search for the points]\label{def:findPoints}
  \uses{def:findPointsWith, def:delivery}
  \lean{Ratpoints.findPoints}
  \leanok
  For the options $s$ of the sieve (Definition~\ref{def:sieveOptions}) and the options $o$
  (Definition~\ref{def:Options}), by default those of a run of the program without options, the list
  of the points that the search with a callback (Definition~\ref{def:findPointsWith}), the model of
  the library function \verb|find_points| with its parts \verb|find_points_work| and
  \verb|find_points_work_1|, delivers to the callback of the model that appends each point to an
  array and never stops the search (Definition~\ref{def:delivery}), from the empty array, or its
  refusal. So it refuses more than $\mathrm{maxDegree} + 1$ coefficients, fewer than two, and $H =
  0$ or $H \ge 2^{63}$, a height that is not a C \verb|long| (Definition~\ref{def:useCLong}), in the
  order of the program, with its errors 3, 5 and~4 (Section~\ref{sec:refusals}; the refusals of
  Definition~\ref{def:reversal}), where the library refuses the first three as bad arguments: a
  degree above $\mathrm{maxDegree}$, and the empty list as the degree $-1$, before it drops the
  leading zeros, and the degree $0$ and a height below $1$ after it, and cannot be given the last,
  its height being a C \verb|long| (\verb|ratpoints.h:362|), which the program refuses when it reads
  it; then, as not squarefree (error~8), a form that loses $\lceil n/2 \rceil$ with the leading
  zeros. Then it normalizes the options (Definition~\ref{def:normalize}) and runs the reversal check
  with the normalized switch (Definition~\ref{def:reversal}), which may refuse the form as not
  squarefree too or reverse the polynomial; it takes the search intervals of the options, the
  default interval $[-h, h]$ for none, and refuses intervals that are not well formed with
  $\mathrm{badArgs}$, error~9 (Definition~\ref{def:searchIntervals}); with the Sturm step on
  (Definition~\ref{def:sturmRegion}), it refuses the form when the remainder chain of the polynomial
  searched is $\mathrm{none}$ (Definition~\ref{def:sturmChain}), lists no point when the step keeps
  no interval, so that that polynomial is negative on the search intervals within $\pm 2^{63}$, and
  it has no point at infinity, and otherwise narrows the search intervals to those that the step
  keeps; and it lists the points of the search of the polynomial that the check hands on
  (Definition~\ref{def:searchPoints}), with its flag of the reversal, over the normalized range of
  the denominators and the search intervals that the Sturm step leaves: the 2-adic information, no
  points when no class of the denominators modulo $64$ admits a numerator and there is no point at
  infinity, the choice of the moduli from the curve (Definition~\ref{def:choice}), no points when
  the curve has none modulo a prime looked at on the way, and else the points at infinity followed,
  unless the Sturm step has left no interval, by the points of the denominators in the order of the
  loop of the search whose $x/z$ lies in a search interval, found in the run over the blocks with
  the correction of the moduli between them (Definition~\ref{def:sieveRun}) with pieces of at most
  $\mathrm{arraySizeAt}(H)$ bit arrays, the array size of $s$ (Definition~\ref{def:arraySize}), each
  mapped back to the caller's curve after a reversal. It models no C code: the search with a
  callback holds the lines of \verb|find_points| that the search for the points held before stage~G.
\end{definition}

Each component differs from the C code in a stated way, recorded in the \verb|Deviation:|
paragraph of the component whose range holds it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the blocks of the run sifted in turn on the calling thread, without the pool of threads,
    which sifts them apart and delivers them in the same order (Theorem~\ref{thm:threads});
  \item the flags of the check as an argument of the points at infinity, for the bits
    \verb|RATPOINTS_REVERSED|, \verb|RATPOINTS_NO_CHECK| and \verb|RATPOINTS_NO_Y| of the flags,
    which the search hands on, the flag of the reversal from the reversal check; the square root of
    $c_d$ by \verb|Nat.sqrt|, for \verb|mpz_sqrt|; and the lines of the report under
    \verb|RATPOINTS_VERBOSE| around them printed by the search (Definition~\ref{def:report});
  \item no failure for lack of memory, which the model cannot meet.
\end{itemize}
$\mathrm{findPoints}$ searches every input that it does not refuse (Theorem~\ref{thm:refusals}):
with the Sturm step off, forms that are not squarefree among them, which ratpoints searches under
\verb|-s|, and without $\mathrm{noCheck}$ it returns their points in the search region of the
options, those with $y < 0$ left out under $\mathrm{noY}$, as Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints} says;
under $\mathrm{noCheck}$ it returns the survivors of the sieve, each with $y = 0$, as
Theorem~\ref{thm:noCheck} says. The points at infinity deliver $(1, 0, 0)$ twice for $c_d = 0$ and
even $d$, as the C code would, which only the zero polynomial meets: ratpoints refuses it before it
gets there, and so does $\mathrm{findPoints}$ since stage~D, so that no input reaches it.

\section{The theorems}\label{sec:sieveTheorems}

\begin{lemma}[Sound filters]\label{lem:flatMap_sound}
  \uses{def:bruteForce}
  \lean{List.flatMap_filter_of_ne, Ratpoints.BruteForce.pointsOfDen_filter_of_ne}
  \leanok
  If every element $x$ of a list with $g(x)$ nonempty passes the test $k$, then filtering the
  list by $k$ before applying $g$ does not change the concatenation of the $g(x)$. In particular a
  filter of the numerators that every point passes does not change what the brute-force reference
  finds over a denominator.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  By induction on the list: an element that fails the test contributes nothing.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Every point survives]\label{lem:survives}
  \uses{def:SieveEntry, def:bruteForce}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.survives_of_mem_pointsOfDen}
  \leanok
  A numerator over which the exact check finds a point for the denominator $b$ survives sound sieve
  entries for $b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The soundness of each entry.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A filter of the outer list moved inside]\label{lem:flatMapFilter}
  \lean{List.flatMap_filter_flatMap_filter}
  \leanok
  For a list $l$, a test $p$ of its elements and a test $T$ of the pairs: the concatenation over the
  elements $b$ of $l$ filtered by $p$ of the elements of a list $g(b)$ that pass $T(b, \cdot)$ is
  the concatenation over the elements $b$ of $l$ of the elements of $g(b)$ that pass
  $p(b) \wedge T(b, \cdot)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  An element of $l$ that fails $p$ gives no elements on the right.
\end{proof}

By this fact on lists the contract of the run of the search (Lemma~\ref{lem:searchList}) moves the
test of the denominators of the listing of a shape, which is the list of the denominators of the
search filtered by a test, into the test of the numerators, a denominator that fails it giving no
numerators. When every denominator over which the brute-force reference finds a point passes these
tests, and every numerator of such a point passes the word of the class of its denominator and the
sieve entries of its block, these tests change nothing that the exact check finds
(Lemma~\ref{lem:flatMap_sound}): with the exact check on, the run delivers the concatenation of what
the reference finds over the denominators of the search and the numerators in their ranges, in the
order of the search, mapped by $\mathrm{swapPoint}$ when the flag of the reversal is set, those with
$y < 0$ left out under $\mathrm{noY}$, which is what the search needs of it.

\begin{theorem}[The points, each once]\label{thm:findPoints}
  \uses{def:findPoints, def:sieveOptions, def:Options, def:IsPoint, def:points, def:regionSpec,
    def:sturmTheorem}
  \lean{Ratpoints.mem_iff_isPoint_of_findPoints_eq_ok, Ratpoints.nodup_of_findPoints_eq_ok, Ratpoints.toFinset_eq_filter_points_of_findPoints_eq_ok}
  \leanok
  For the options $s$ of the sieve and $o$, if $\mathrm{findPoints}(c, H, s, o)$ returns the list
  $L$: assuming Sturm's theorem when the Sturm step is on (Definition~\ref{def:sturmTheorem}), for
  $\mathrm{noCheck}$ false, a triple lies in $L$ if and only if it is a normalized point of height
  at most $H$, with $y \ge 0$ under $\mathrm{noY}$, in the search region of $o$
  (Definition~\ref{def:regionSpec}), and its set of elements is $\mathrm{points}(c, H)$ filtered by
  the search region when both options of the output are off; and $L$ has no duplicates, for every
  option, without that assumption.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{thm:findPoints_perm, thm:bruteForce, thm:findPoints_order, lem:swapPoint_bijection,
    lem:findPoints_searchList, lem:noCheckList, lem:regionFilter, lem:elimSearchRegion, lem:choice}
  \leanok
  The search runs with the result of the choice of the moduli, which has what the run needs
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:choice}), whatever moduli it chooses. Without $\mathrm{noCheck}$ and assuming
  Sturm's theorem, $L$ is the list of the reference of the polynomial searched over the candidate
  $(1, 0)$ and the denominators of its search up to the largest denominator, mapped back after a
  reversal and filtered by $\mathrm{noY}$ and the search region (Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_order},
  Lemmas~\ref{lem:elimSearchRegion} and~\ref{lem:regionFilter}), and that list before the filters is
  a permutation of the points of $\mathrm{bruteForce}(c, H)$ at infinity or with a denominator up to
  the largest, the only ones in the search region (Lemma~\ref{lem:swapPoint_bijection}, as in
  Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_perm}), so that Theorem~\ref{thm:bruteForce} gives membership, no
  duplicates and, with both options of the output off, the set of elements. Under
  $\mathrm{noCheck}$, $L$ is the list that the search delivers for a sound test
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:findPoints_searchList}), whose survivors have no duplicates
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:noCheckList}).
\end{proof}
