\chapter{The plain sieve}\label{chap:sieve}

Stage~C1 replaces the brute-force reference in the program by a sieve in the skeleton of the C
function \verb|find_points_work_1|, with the simplest component in each place: the denominators
$1, \dots, H$ in order, the numerators $-H, \dots, H$, one Boolean per numerator, prime moduli
with tables of admissible residues, the test for common factors, the exact check, the delivery of
$\pm y$, and the points at infinity computed directly from $d$ and $c_d$. Every row of the sieve is
\emph{sound}: it lets every point through. So for every well-formed choice of the sieving
parameters the sieve returns exactly the list of the brute-force reference, the same points in the
same order, and membership and no duplicates follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:bruteForce}.

The later stages contribute in four ways. A \emph{data refinement} computes the same predicate in
another way, with a lemma that it does: the bit arrays of words of stage~C2, the faithful tables of
stage~F1, the stages of the sieve and the check of stage~F2, whose third stage tests the rows of
moduli that the sieve applies already, and the packing of stage~F3; the equation with the reference
survives it. A \emph{transformation}, the reversal of stage~D, is a bijection of the point sets that
preserves the box. A \emph{sound filter} comes with the lemma that every point passes it: the 2-adic
information and the tests on the denominators of stage~E and the composite moduli of stage~F1. An
\emph{enumeration refinement} visits the denominators that carry points in another order: the loop
shapes of stage~E. From the reversal on, and with the divisor order of stage~E, the statement is
that the output is a permutation of the reference, which still gives membership and no duplicates;
since stage~E a theorem also gives its order, that of the reference over the denominators in the
order of the loop of the search (Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_order}), which stage~G spells out in
terms of the polynomial.

\section{What a default run of the C code does}\label{sec:skeleton}

\verb|find_points| (\csource{find_points.c}{5226}{5242}) calls \verb|find_points_work|, which
refuses a degree outside $0, \dots, \mathrm{maxDegree}$ (\csource{find_points.c}{4557}{4558}) and
calls
\verb|find_points_work_1|. In a run without options, that function:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item drops the leading zero coefficients, refusing the form as not squarefree when that lowers
    $\lceil n/2 \rceil$, refuses degree $0$ and a height below $1$, and caps the parameters
    (\csource{find_points.c}{4624}{4658}); it reverses the polynomial in some cases, refusing the
    form as not squarefree for even $d$ with $c_0 = c_1 = 0$ (\csource{find_points.c}{4732}{4803};
    stage~D);
  \item computes the remainder chain of $f$ and $f'$ of the Sturm step, refusing the form as not
    squarefree when a remainder is zero (\csource{sturm.c}{99}{163},
    \csource{find_points.c}{4857}{4892}; stage~D), and from it the search region (stage~H), and
    decides whether there are points at infinity (\csource{find_points.c}{4894}{4899});
  \item sets up the tests on the denominators (squares or divisors times squares, Jacobi symbols)
    and the 2-adic information, with an exit when no class of denominators modulo $64$ admits a
    numerator (stage~E);
  \item chooses the moduli and the forbidden divisors, examining each prime: the table
    \verb|is_f_square| of the residues $x$ modulo $p$ for which $f(x)$ is a square modulo $p$
    (\csource{find_points.c}{1703}{1747}), with an exit when there are no points modulo $p$
    (stage~D);
  \item delivers the points at infinity (\csource{find_points.c}{5068}{5105});
  \item runs over the denominators in blocks (\csource{find_points.c}{5152}{5160}); for each
    denominator $b$ that passes its tests, it sieves the numerators of the search region in pieces
    of bit arrays with the rows of the moduli of the first two stages (\verb|sift| and
    \verb|_ratpoints_sift0|), tests each survivor for common factors with $b$
    (\csource{sift.c}{318}{343}), then with the primes of the third stage one numerator at a time,
    and checks the rest exactly, delivering $(a, y, b)$ and, for $y \ne 0$, $(a, -y, b)$
    (\csource{find_points.c}{5250}{5338}).
\end{enumerate}
C1 models what decides which points are found, each in its simplest form. Most of the rest only
spares work; the refusal of a form that is not squarefree, the reversal and the exit when there
are no points modulo $p$ (D) and the options of the output (G) come in their stages.

\verb|Params| holds the choices that the theorems quantify over: which moduli the sieve uses, in
which order and in which stage, and how many primes are examined. ratpoints makes them from the
curve (\verb|sieving_info|, which ranks the primes and may look past the first $q$ when \verb|-p| is
absent), and moves the end of the third stage during the run (\verb|adapt_primes|; with \verb|-A 2|
also $s_2$); where it decides by the curve, for an absent \verb|-n|, \verb|-N| or \verb|-P|, its
stages may be longer or shorter than those of the parameters of the executable, and where the
options fix their counts, they are at most as long, unless \verb|-R| raises the offset of the second
stage (Section~\ref{sec:cuts}). None of this changes the points found or their order, since a
modulus only removes candidates that are not points, a test on the denominators (the forbidden
divisors among the examined primes and beyond them, at most the bound of them, and the Jacobi test
that the switch lets run) removes only denominators without points, and the two shapes of the loop
between which these choices decide, the words of 64 denominators and every denominator, both run in
increasing order (\verb|-q| suppresses the report of the moduli used, which does depend on the
choice). So the model takes the choice as an input, and the theorems hold for every well-formed
choice. They quantify over the parameters that \verb|Params.WF| admits, a definition of a few lines
in the model, which has no Mathlib, so the comparator challenge imports it; the runs of the
executable need no such reading, since \verb|wf_sieveParams|, certified with the main theorems,
shows that every parameter set the command line produces is well-formed, which also rules out a
well-formedness that nothing satisfies. C1 reads only the moduli and treats all stages alike, and C2
reads the array size too (Definition~\ref{def:arraySize}); the bound on the forbidden divisors and
the Jacobi switch are read since stage~E, the number of primes examined since stage~F1
(Chapter~\ref{chap:tables}), and the cut points from stage~F2 on, and further choices (the block
length) join \verb|Params| with the components that use them. Well-formedness constrains a field
only as far as its component needs it to be meaningful. The constants of the build that these
definitions read, the size in bits of the moduli, the defaults of \verb|-p| and \verb|-F| and those
of the cut points, are definitions of the model like $\mathrm{maxDegree}$
(Definition~\ref{def:maxDegree}), and no proof depends on their values: \verb|wf_sieveParams| needs
only that $\mathrm{numPrimes}$, the number of primes of the table below $\mathrm{maxPrimeEven}$, is
at most its size, and that these primes come first in the table, which is sorted
(Definition~\ref{def:barrett}): the moduli that the options give are among them, each a prime whose
only divisor above $1$ is itself.

\section{The table of a prime}\label{sec:primeTables}

Write $f = \sum_{i \le d} c_i x^i$ for the polynomial of degree $d$. For a prime $p$ and a
denominator $b$ not divisible by $p$, $F_c(a, b) \equiv b^D f(a b^{-1}) \pmod p$, and $b^D$ is the
square of a unit since $D$ is even; so if $F_c(a, b) = y^2$, then $f(a b^{-1})$ is a square
modulo $p$ (zero included). If $p > 1$ divides $b$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then $p$ does not divide
$a$. These two facts make sound the rows that the tables below define.

\begin{definition}[Squares and inverses modulo a prime]\label{def:squaresMod}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.squaresMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.inversesMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_squaresMod_mul_self_mod, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_inversesMod_mul_mod}
  \leanok
  The table of the squares modulo $p$, made as the generator of the tables of ratpoints makes it:
  zero, and the squares of the odd residues (\csource{gen_find_points_h.c}{69}{88}); and the
  table of the inverses modulo $p$ of the residues $1, \dots, p - 1$, each the least positive one
  found by trial (\csource{gen_find_points_h.c}{33}{44}, \csource{gen_find_points_h.c}{90}{100}).
  The squares of all residues are marked, and $r \cdot r^{-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ for a prime $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The table of a prime]\label{def:examinePrime}
  \uses{def:squaresMod}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.evalMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.Entry, Ratpoints.Sieve.examinePrime, Ratpoints.Sieve.natCast_evalMod, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_isFSquare_examinePrime_of_isSquare, Ratpoints.Sieve.getElem!_isFSquare_examinePrime_self}
  \leanok
  For a prime $p$: for each residue $x$ modulo $p$ whether $f(x)$ is a square modulo $p$, with
  $f(x)$ computed by Horner's rule modulo $p$ from the coefficients reduced modulo $p$ by the
  Euclidean remainder, and as the entry $p$ whether there is a point at infinity modulo $p$, for odd
  $d$ or $c_d$ a square modulo $p$: $p + 1$ entries, as \verb|examine_prime| keeps them; kept, as
  the sieve entry of ratpoints keeps it (\csource{rp-private.h}{542}{555}), together with $p$ and
  the inverses modulo $p$. The table is made for every prime it is given. It is the reference of the
  table with the exit (Definition~\ref{def:examinePrimeExit}), which models \verb|examine_prime|
  with its two paths through \verb|long|s and GMP integers: that gives no table at a prime modulo
  which the curve has no points, and this one at every other prime below $\mathrm{maxPrimeEven}$.
  Ratpoints also makes no table for a prime at which $f(x)$ is a square for every residue and there
  is no point at infinity modulo $p$, which it leaves out of the moduli.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The rows of a denominator]\label{def:admits}
  \uses{def:examinePrime}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.Entry.admits}
  \leanok
  The row of the denominator $b$ in the table of $p$ admits the numerator $a$ when, for the residues
  $r = b \bmod p$ and $x = a \bmod p$, the table's entry at $x r^{-1} \bmod p$ is true if $r \ne 0$,
  and $x \ne 0$ if $r = 0$. This is what the bit of $a$ says in the row of $r$ in ratpoints, where
  the rows are bit patterns built from the table by a function for each prime that the build
  generates (Definitions~\ref{def:sieveInit1} and~\ref{def:sieveInit2}) and, for $r = 0$, a fixed
  pattern that does not depend on the curve (Definition~\ref{def:sieves0}); the admission test, with
  no C range of its own, is what these rows are specified by, through the closed form
  (Definition~\ref{def:closedRow}). The table of a prime is the one factor of the sieve
  entry of the prime (Definition~\ref{def:SieveEntry}), whose row is this row; a numerator survives
  the moduli for $b$ when the row of $b$ in the sieve entry of every modulus admits it, the
  interface between the sieve and the check, against which the bit arrays of stage~C2 are specified
  (Chapter~\ref{chap:words}).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[A square value of the form gives a square value of $f$]\label{lem:isSquare_div}
  \uses{def:binaryForm}
  \lean{IsUnit.isSquare_of_sq_eq_mul_pow, Ratpoints.sum_mul_pow_mul_pow_sub_of_eq_mul, Ratpoints.isSquare_sum_mul_pow_of_sq_eq_binaryForm, Ratpoints.isSquare_sum_mul_div_pow_of_sq_eq}
  \leanok
  In a commutative monoid, if $y^2 = v u^D$ with $u$ a unit and $D$ even, then $v$ is a square. In a
  commutative semiring, if $d \le D$ and $a = k b$, then
  $\sum_{i \le d} f_i\, a^i b^{D - i} = b^D \sum_{i \le d} f_i\, k^i$. So if $y^2 = F_c(a, b)$ for
  integers $a$, $y$ and a natural number $b$ whose image in a commutative ring $R$ is a unit, then
  $f(k)$ is a square in $R$ for the $k$ with $a = k b$ in $R$. In a semifield likewise, if
  $b \ne 0$, $D$ is even, $d \le D$ and $y^2 = \sum_{i \le d} f_i\, a^i b^{D - i}$, then
  $\sum_{i \le d} f_i\, (a/b)^i$ is a square.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The form is $b^D \sum_{i \le d} f_i\, k^i$, and $b^D$ is the square of the unit $b^{D/2}$, so the
  sum is the square of $y b^{-D/2}$.
\end{proof}

Mathlib has the identity behind this in the form of polynomials, \verb|Polynomial.eval_homogenize|;
the sum form, with its few lines of proof, suits the definition of $F_c$.

\begin{lemma}[The rows let every point through]\label{lem:admits_examinePrime}
  \uses{def:admits, def:binaryForm, def:bruteForce}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.admits_examinePrime_of_sq_eq}
  \leanok
  Let $p$ be a prime, $b$ a natural number, $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $y^2 = F_c(a, b)$. Then the row
  of $b$ in the table of $p$ for the degree of $c$ admits $a$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{lem:isSquare_div}
  \leanok
  The tables are what they claim to be: the squares, the inverses and Horner's rule modulo $p$
  compute their definitions. For $p \nmid b$, cast $y^2 = F_c(a, b)$ to $\ZZ/p\ZZ$ and apply
  Lemma~\ref{lem:isSquare_div}; the table of squares then has the entry at $a b^{-1}$. For
  $p \mid b$, a common divisor $p > 1$ of $a$ and $b$ would divide $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, so
  $p \nmid a$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:admits_examinePrime} needs neither $b \ge 1$ nor an odd prime, so at its use the
sieve's denominators and the primes of the table need no side conditions.

\section{The sieve}\label{sec:sieve}

The model follows the rules of the translation throughout. A C \verb|long| is an \verb|Int| or a
\verb|Nat|, except in the test for common factors and in the bits of an interval of numerators,
which compute on the 64-bit words of Lean core as the C code does (Definitions~\ref{def:relprime}
and~\ref{def:numeratorsToBits}, Lemmas~\ref{lem:relprime} and~\ref{lem:numeratorsToBits}); the
reductions by a reciprocal cast their arguments to 64-bit words, as the macros cast them to
\verb|unsigned long|s, and take the high half of the 128-bit product from the products of the 32-bit
halves (Definitions~\ref{def:mulMod} and~\ref{def:mulHi}); the obligation that a value lies in the
range of its type is a lemma of the faithful component that computes in \verb|long|s: since stage~D
for the helper \verb|mod| and the negations of the map of a reversed search
(Definition~\ref{def:useCLong}), and from stage~F on for the faithful components of the sieve (in
stage~F2 the bounds of the third stage, Lemma~\ref{lem:stage3}, and in stage~F3 those of the
packing, Lemma~\ref{lem:packingLongs}). The remainder \verb|mod()| of the C code is Lean's Euclidean
\verb|%|, but in the table of a prime with the exit and the tables of a prime power, which model it
as it is and prove it equal to \verb|%| (Definitions~\ref{def:useCLong} and~\ref{def:examinePower}).
The tables that the build of ratpoints generates (the squares, the inverses, the row of $0$) are
Lean functions with lemmas that they compute what they claim, checked against the generated header
by \verb|#guard| examples; the row of $0$ is the closed form of its admission test
(Lemma~\ref{lem:sieves0}). Each loop of the sieve is a definition of type \verb|Id α| that its
callers bind with \verb|←|, so that its contract, an \verb|@[spec]| theorem proved with the program
logic of Lean core and with loop invariants that are named definitions, applies at the call; only
\verb|findPoints| runs a component with \verb|Id.run|. The reference table of a prime
(Definition~\ref{def:examinePrime}) is made of folds and maps, specified by ordinary lemmas; the
table with the exit and the tables of a prime power, which loop over the residues, are components of
type \verb|Id| with their contracts (Lemmas~\ref{lem:examinePrime_eq} and~\ref{lem:power_admits}).

\begin{definition}[The sieve of one denominator]\label{def:sieveDen}
  \uses{def:SieveEntry}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.sieveDen, Ratpoints.Sieve.sieveDen_spec}
  \leanok
  An array of $2H + 1$ Booleans, one per numerator $a = i - H$, all true at the start; for the sieve
  entry of each modulus in turn, every entry is replaced by its conjunction with the row of $b$ at
  $a$. Its contract: entry $i$ is the survivor test of the numerator $i - H$. These are the ANDs of
  the first and the second stage of ratpoints, with the masks at the ends of the range, and the test
  of its third stage, in their simplest form; with the check of Definition~\ref{def:checkDen} the
  sieve stays in the model as the reference of the bit arrays of words of Chapter~\ref{chap:words},
  which model that code (Lemma~\ref{lem:sift_eq_checkDen}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The survivors of one denominator]\label{def:checkDen}
  \uses{def:bruteForce}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.checkDen, Ratpoints.Sieve.checkDen_spec}
  \leanok
  The numerators whose entry in a given array of Booleans is true, in increasing order, each tested
  for common factors with $b$ and checked exactly: $F_c(a, b)$ by Horner's rule on the products
  $c_k b^{d-k}$, computed once per denominator, times $b$ once more when $d$ is odd, then
  $(a, y, b)$ and, for $y \ne 0$, $(a, -y, b)$ when $F_c(a, b) = y^2$ with $y \ge 0$; the exact
  check is that of the brute-force reference. Its contract: if entry $i$ is $k(i - H)$ for every
  $i$, for a test $k$ of the numerators, then the result is what the brute-force reference finds
  over the denominator $b$ and the numerators $-H, \dots, H$ that pass $k$. These are the scan and
  the extraction of ratpoints with the test for common factors and the exact check, in their
  simplest form; on the sieve of Definition~\ref{def:sieveDen} the check stays in the model as the
  reference of the bit arrays of words of Chapter~\ref{chap:words}, which model that code
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:sift_eq_checkDen}).
\end{definition}

  \uses{def:sift, def:Shape, lem:shape_dens, def:stage3, def:fillBpList, lem:fillBpList,

Each component differs from the C code in a stated way, recorded in the \verb|Deviation:|
paragraph of the component whose range holds it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the denominators of a shape listed, then tested and sifted one after the other, each in the
    packing of its class modulo $64$, from $1$ to $H$, for the denominator range (H), the blocks
    (I), and the threads, which deliver the blocks in the same order;
  \item every numerator from $-H$ to $H$ in the packing of the class of the denominator, for the
    search region (H);
  \item the cut points of the three stages fixed for the run, for their correction between the
    blocks of the run from the counts of the survivors (I);
  \item the flag of the reversal as an argument of the points at infinity, for the bit
    \verb|RATPOINTS_REVERSED| of the flags, which the search hands on from the reversal check of
    $\mathrm{findPoints}$; and a list of points for the callback \verb|process|, with no stop it may
    request, no \verb|NO_Y| and no \verb|NO_CHECK| (G), and no output under \verb|VERBOSE|;
  \item no failure for lack of memory, which the model cannot meet.
\end{itemize}
$\mathrm{findPoints}$ searches every input that it does not refuse (Theorem~\ref{thm:refusals}):
with the Sturm step off, forms that are not squarefree among them, which ratpoints searches under
\verb|-s|, and it returns their points, as Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints} says for every option. The
points at infinity have the case $y = 0$ for $c_d = 0$ and even $d$, which only the zero polynomial
meets; ratpoints refuses it before it gets there, and so does $\mathrm{findPoints}$ since stage~D.

\section{The theorems}\label{sec:sieveTheorems}

\begin{lemma}[Sound filters]\label{lem:flatMap_sound}
  \uses{def:bruteForce}
  \lean{List.flatMap_filter_of_ne, Ratpoints.BruteForce.pointsOfDen_filter_of_ne}
  \leanok
  If every element $x$ of a list with $g(x)$ nonempty passes the test $k$, then filtering the
  list by $k$ before applying $g$ does not change the concatenation of the $g(x)$. In particular a
  filter of the numerators that every point passes does not change what the brute-force reference
  finds over a denominator.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  By induction on the list: an element that fails the test contributes nothing.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Every point survives]\label{lem:survives}
  \uses{def:SieveEntry, def:bruteForce}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.survives_of_mem_pointsOfDen}
  \leanok
  A numerator over which the exact check finds a point for the denominator $b$ survives sound sieve
  entries for $b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The soundness of each entry.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The run and the reference]\label{thm:run_reference}
  \uses{def:sieveRun, def:bruteForce, def:stage3, lem:admits_mul, lem:classOffsets, lem:numClasses}
  \lean{Ratpoints.Sieve.toList_run_sieveRun_eq_flatMap}
  \leanok
  For an array size $A \ge 1$, any cut points, sieve entries with their reciprocals, the first $s_2$
  with their tables of inverses and the row offsets of the classes with a pattern, whose bits are
  those of \verb|den_bits|, and those from $s_2$ on prime entries (Definition~\ref{def:stage3}),
  2-adic words that the classes pack, denominators from $b_{\mathrm{low}} \ge 1$ to
  $b_{\mathrm{high}} < 2^{63}$, $H < 2^{63}$ and a list $l$ of denominators such that the list of
  the shape is $l$ filtered by a test, every denominator of $l$ over which the brute-force reference
  finds a point passes that test and the test of the shape, and every numerator of such a point
  passes the word of the class of its denominator and the sieve entries:
  the run gives the concatenation of what the reference finds over the denominators of $l$, in the
  order of $l$, mapped by $\mathrm{swapPoint}$ when the flag of the reversal is set.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{lem:flatMap_sound}
  \leanok
  By the contract of the denominators (Definition~\ref{def:sieveRun}) the run gives the
  concatenation, over the denominators of the list of the shape that pass its test, of what the
  reference finds over the numerators that pass the word of the class of the denominator and survive
  the sieve entries, mapped when the flag is set. Since every numerator of a point passes these
  filters, and every denominator of a point of $l$ passes the test of the list and that of the
  shape, the filters change nothing (Lemma~\ref{lem:flatMap_sound}).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The points, each once]\label{thm:findPoints}
  \uses{def:findPoints, def:Params, def:Options, def:IsPoint, def:points}
  \lean{Ratpoints.mem_iff_isPoint_of_findPoints_eq_ok, Ratpoints.nodup_of_findPoints_eq_ok, Ratpoints.toFinset_eq_points_of_findPoints_eq_ok}
  \leanok
  For well-formed parameters and every option, if $\mathrm{findPoints}(c, H, P, o)$ returns the list
  $L$, then a triple lies in $L$ if and only if it is a normalized point of height at most $H$, $L$
  has no duplicates, and its set of elements is $\mathrm{points}(c, H)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \uses{thm:findPoints_perm, thm:bruteForce}
  \leanok
  $L$ is a permutation of $\mathrm{bruteForce}(c, H)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_perm}), and
  Theorem~\ref{thm:bruteForce} gives the rest.
\end{proof}

\verb|mem_iff_isPoint_of_findPoints_eq_ok| and \verb|nodup_of_findPoints_eq_ok| are the main
theorems of the project; since stage~D they take the options of \verb|findPoints| as an argument,
and from stage~H on they take the hypothesis of Sturm's theorem. The reversal of stage~D and the
divisor order of stage~E change the order of the output, so that the list is that of the reference
of the polynomial searched over the denominators of its search, in their order, mapped back
(Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_order}), a permutation of the reference
(Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_perm}), and the refusals are those of Theorem~\ref{thm:refusals}.
Comparator certifies the two main theorems, the refusal theorems of stage~D, which rule out a
\verb|findPoints| that refuses every input, with or without the Sturm step, and
\verb|wf_sieveParams|, which rules out a well-formedness that nothing satisfies (it accepted these
statements on 2026-10-11, again on the model of stage E on 2026-10-12, with the parameters of
sub-stage F1 on 2026-10-13, with the refusal of the heights that are not \verb|long|s and the cut
points of sub-stage F2 on 2026-10-14, and with the packing of sub-stage F3 on 2026-10-15): the
challenge states them with \verb|sorry| over \verb|findPoints|, \verb|Options|, \verb|Params|,
\verb|Params.WF|, \verb|SieveOptions|, \verb|sieveParams| and \verb|maxDegree|, imported from the
model, never restated, since a definition of the model elaborates without Mathlib to other instance
terms. Each main theorem has a guard on its axioms, which are the standard ones. The contracts of
the sieve of stages C1 to D need only the program logic of Lean core and stay in files without
Mathlib, so that their \verb|grind| calls see only the lemmas of core, untouched by Mathlib's
updates; the contract of the run over the denominators of a shape, with its plain equation
\verb|toList_run_sieveRun|, is in the file of the run, with Mathlib, like the other contracts of
stage~E (Chapter~\ref{chap:denominators}) and those of the tables and the moduli of stage~F1, which
sit next to the lemmas they serve (Chapter~\ref{chap:tables}); those of the stages of sub-stage~F2
and of the packing of sub-stage~F3 are in files without Mathlib again (Chapters~\ref{chap:stages}
and~\ref{chap:packing}). The soundness of the rows needs Mathlib, through $\ZZ/p\ZZ$.

\section{The command-line program and the differential tests}\label{sec:sieveCli}

\verb|ratpoints-lean| reads the values of \verb|-p|, \verb|-n|, \verb|-N|, \verb|-P|, \verb|-F| and
\verb|-j|/\verb|-J| into \verb|SieveOptions|, with the checks of \verb|main.c| unchanged, and runs
\verb|findPoints| with \verb|sieveParams| of them; the other options stay as in stage~B, until
stage~D reads \verb|-k|, \verb|-K|, \verb|-s| and \verb|-S| (Section~\ref{sec:setupCli}). So the
reading of the options (Definition~\ref{def:cliInput}) returns these values from now on, and the
program (Definition~\ref{def:cliRun}) prints the points of the sieve. Since these are those of the
brute-force reference, every differential test of stage~B applies unchanged, and the brute force
needs no test of its own: \verb|findPoints| returns its list, without a reversal and where the
search visits the denominators in increasing order (Theorem~\ref{thm:findPoints_order}). Compiled,
the plain sieve takes $0.16$~seconds at $H = 1000$, $1.3$ at $H = 3000$ and $3.6$ at $H = 5000$ on a
curve of degree~$6$ with the 30 default moduli, against $0.3$ to $0.9$~seconds for the reference at
$H = 1000$; so the differential tests of stage~C1 searched up to height $5000$, with a suite of
point-rich curves at the heights $2000$ and $5000$, and the bit arrays of stage~C2 raise these
heights (Section~\ref{sec:wordsCli}). A further suite runs both programs with other parameters (no
moduli, all 53 primes and a cap above them, the cut points in several positions, a third stage, no
Jacobi test, no forbidden divisors and many, and negative values, which mean the defaults) on
point-rich and on random curves; since stage~F2 it also has option sets of \verb|-n|, \verb|-N| and
\verb|-P| that run the second and the third stage after few moduli or none in the first, at its
small heights, since most numerators then reach the test of a candidate, which the model runs
numerator by numerator, as the C code does but more slowly (Chapter~\ref{chap:stages}).
